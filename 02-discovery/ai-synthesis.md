# AI Synthesis, Product Health & Insights Summary (Module 2)

## Responses
- **Moment of misery / red flag #1 (e.g., "user gave up after 3 tries"):** **The in-store gap.** Pay-later is not available when Lena wants it. In a physical shop Riverty is not offered, so she pays by debit and the money leaves her account before she has decided to keep the item.

- **Moment of misery / red flag #2:** **The payday squeeze.** A salaried user with no cushion has three payments due two days before payday. Her balance is tight, so she moves money from savings and feels stressed. External evidence supports this: BaFin reports that 32% of BNPL users aged 30–45 have paid an instalment late at some point — Lena, at 32, sits in that band.

- **Moment of misery / red flag #3:** **Lost track of what's due.** She has several open BNPL purchases across Klarna, PayPal and Riverty, each with its own app and due date. BaFin reports that 14% of BNPL users have lost track of open payments, rising to one in four among users under 30.

- **Product Health & Insights Summary (Claude's output):** Riverty's checkout product is technically sound and its platform foundation (Mastercard membership, Paymentology processing) is already paid for, so stability is not the problem. The weakness is experiential: users with several BNPL providers have no single view of what they owe, cannot use pay-later in stores, and choose Riverty only when it happens to appear, which holds brand preference at about 6%. Because nothing is visibly broken, the problem is easy to underweight while Klarna builds everyday card habits in Germany that a checkout-only product cannot match.

- **Did the AI catch the specific moment of misery / pain point you found in Step 1?:** Partly. It identified the in-store gap and the fragmented-repayments problem, both of which match what I found. But it described them at the level of product features ("no single view", "no in-store option") rather than at the level of Lena's actual moment — paying by debit in a shop and having the money leave her account before she knows whether she will keep the item.

- **Did it smooth over a critical frustration into a generic bullet point?:** Yes. It split the customer problems into neat separate headings and bullet points, which made them look equally weighted. In reality the in-store gap is the one that breaks the core promise of pay-later; the others are irritations around it. The tidy structure hid that hierarchy, and I had to restore it myself.

- **Did the AI try to suggest features or a roadmap despite the constraints?:** No, not at this stage. It stayed within diagnosis.

- **Logic leak / hallucination #1 (e.g., "AI suggested a new search bar feature, roadmap leak"):** **The traveller or weekend shopper.** The AI introduced a user abroad or at a weekend market who cannot use BNPL at all and carries a debit card instead. This segment does not appear anywhere in the brief — the AI invented it. It is plausible, which is what makes it dangerous: an invented segment can quietly become a design target without any evidence behind it.

- **Logic leak / hallucination #2:** **The deliberate avoider.** The AI introduced a user who is wary of credit cards after a bad experience with debt and likes BNPL because it feels controlled. This is also not in the brief. However, it is a useful counter-case rather than a target: if the card does not feel as simple and safe as BNPL, this user will not adopt it. I keep it as a hypothesis to test in discovery, not as a finding.
