# Problem Hook & Value Proposition (Module 1)

## Responses
- **Chosen path, which scenario are you committing to? (StreamLine or RouteLogic, or your own):** Riverty's First Consumer Card — a Riverty Mastercard for existing BNPL users in Germany, starting as a friends-and-family MVP.

- **Strategic crisis, based on the brief, if the company does nothing for 12 months, what happens to its market position?:** The cost of twelve months is not lost revenue — year-one volumes are small either way. It is that the window to enter as a differentiated player closes. Klarna Card grew from 1.3 million to 6.5 million active users in a year, and every month Riverty's own heavy users build an everyday card habit around someone else's brand. Riverty would then enter later, as a follower, into a market where the habit has already formed.

- **Moment of misery, what is the user likely doing outside the product (Google, spreadsheets, a competitor) because your tool isn't serving them?:** For Lena, the honest answer is that she is not working around Riverty — she is simply using something else and thinking nothing of it.

  She reaches for Klarna or PayPal, which are already on her phone. The brief says she uses whichever option appears at checkout and rarely goes looking for Riverty. In a physical shop she has no pay-later option at all and pays by debit. So the behaviour is not a workaround — it is indifference. That is worse than friction, because friction implies she wanted Riverty and couldn't get there.

- **Problem hook, summarize the business risk and user pain into one urgent sentence.:** Riverty's most loyal users carry Klarna and PayPal for everything outside its merchant base — and every month without a card is a month those habits harden around someone else's brand, in the market where the gap is already widest.

- **Value proposition, based on the proposed initiative (Spotlight or Velocity), what is the new value and why is it urgent to launch now?:** **"Keep it first, pay later — anywhere."** Lena can use pay-later in stores and beyond Riverty's partner merchants, with one place to see what is due and settle on time. Returns made before repayment is due net off her balance, so money only leaves her account for things she has decided to keep. Riverty gets a direct, everyday relationship with its users instead of being only a checkout option.

  **How it earns money:** EU interchange on consumer credit cards is capped at 0.30%, which on the brief's year-one volume of €125m is roughly €375k — not enough to carry the product. Revenue therefore has to come mainly from interest on optional revolving balances (a Flex-style upgrade after the interest-free period), with interchange as a contributor. This makes the card a lending product with a payment rail, not a payments product.

  **Why launch now:**
  1. **CCD2 changes the economics of checkout credit.** From 20 November 2026, every in-scope BNPL purchase at checkout requires a full creditworthiness assessment covering income, expenses and existing debts — including BNPL with other providers — plus pre-contractual information. Checkout BNPL pays that cost on every transaction. A card pays it once, at onboarding, and then serves every purchase. CCD2 does not give a card an exemption; it gives it a structural advantage.
  2. **Klarna is compounding.** Klarna Card grew fivefold in a year, and Germany is Klarna's largest market by volume.
  3. **The platform is already paid for.** Riverty holds a Mastercard principal membership with Paymentology as processor, funded by the Amazon Business pilot. The marginal cost of a second programme is far lower than the first.
  4. **Capacity is the real constraint.** The Amazon Business pilot (live October 2026) is absorbing engineering, risk and operations capacity. Without a committed slot now, the consumer card slips by default to whichever workstream pushes hardest.

  **Load-bearing assumption (to confirm with Legal):** that drawdowns on a card credit line assessed at onboarding do not each trigger a fresh CCD2 creditworthiness assessment at the point of purchase. If this does not hold, reason 1 weakens significantly.

## Cold-read your own hook
- **Is the business risk high enough to justify a new initiative, a high-stakes threat, or a minor inconvenience?:** High-stakes, but slow-moving — which is exactly why it is easy to underweight. Nothing in Riverty's checkout product is visibly broken, so there is no incident to force action. The threat is that brand preference stays at around 6% while Klarna turns its BNPL users into daily cardholders. The cost shows up as a lost position rather than lost revenue, and it is far cheaper to act before the habit forms than after.

- **Is the moment of misery a systemic problem or just an edge case?:** Systemic. It applies to every Riverty user every time they shop outside a partner merchant — which includes every physical shop. It is supported by the brief's three structural gaps: no in-store presence, under-monetised heavy users, and weak brand pull. It is not limited to a niche segment; the friction is built into a checkout-only model.

- **Does the value proposition actually remove the obstacle you identified, or is it a generic feature?:** It removes the obstacle directly, but only if "keep it first" is genuinely delivered, not just claimed. On a card, the merchant is paid immediately; what protects Lena is that her repayment is deferred and that refunds net off her balance before anything is due. If refund matching is slow or unreliable, she pays and then waits for her money back — which recreates the exact problem she has with debit. This is why refund netting is treated as part of the core proposition in the roadmap, not as a later enhancement.
